“You’ve just missed it.”

I looked up from my phone. A red bus was disappearing around the corner. But, I really couldn’t make the number.

“The 143?” I asked.

She nodded in confirmation.

I lowered the hand I’d raised too late. Then I put my phone into my pocket.

She was sitting at one end of the bench, her jet black hair tied in a ponytail, and the sleeves of her shirt folded a little bit.

“He could’ve waited.” I told.

“He did.”

I glanced down the road, though the bus was gone.

“Oh.”

She moved over a little. I checked the seat for rainwater before sitting down.

The rain was letting up, but the roof of the bus stop was still dripping. I remembered my umbrella as soon as I stepped outside the office. It was neatly stowed away inside my locker. I wanted to go back for it but then decided I’d rather get wet. My manager had already kept me for twenty minutes. If he saw me again, he’d find something else. Which I don’t have the mental capacity to deal with at the moment.

And then my phone began to vibrate.

His name was on the screen. I silenced the call.

“That kind of a day?” she asked.

“Apparently it’s not over.”

I put the phone away and lowered my bag between my feet. My shoulder ached where the strap had been.

Across the road, the shopkeeper lifted his sagging fabric awning with a pole. A sheet of rainwater spilled off the edge, narrowly missing a man on the pavement. The man jumped back and shouted. Whatever the shopkeeper said in reply was lost in the sound of traffic.

“He always does that, you know.” she said.

“Empty it onto people?” I was confused.

“Only when someone’s passing. He’s had all evening.”

“Maybe he waits for them.”

“No. Look at his face.”

The shopkeeper stood with the pole in his hand, looking offended.

I laughed.

She smiled and settled back against the bench.

This is where I got on to but for three years now. Since starting my job here, I have been a daily commuter from here. I knew that shop. I’d bought stuff from there, asked for change, waited under its smaller fabric awning when the bus stop was full. I couldn’t remember a day watching the man close up.

“Do you catch your bus here too?” I asked.

“I used to.”

“Where do you..”

“Your bag’s getting wet.”

Water was trickling under the bench. I pulled the bag back on to my lap and checked for the damage. I needed to wipe the water off but the only thing I could find in my pockets to wipe it with was an old receipt.

“I’d let it dry first,” she said.

I looked at the receipt, then put it away.

For a while, we watched the road. A radio was playing in the shop, barely loud enough to hear between passing vehicles. She shifted her feet away from the water dripping off the roof.

Usually I would have reached for my phone by then. It saved me from wondering whether I ought to say something.

But she seemed content to sit there, and after a minute I stopped trying to think of a question.

A dog wandered up to the shop. It stood in the doorway until the shopkeeper noticed it, then sat down.

“He’s hopeful,” I said.

“He gets fed.”

“Every night?”

“Most nights.”

The man went inside and returned with something wrapped in newspaper. He tore off a piece and dropped it beside the dog.

“Is that a bun?”

“Bread.”

“How can you tell from here?”

“It’s always bread.”

“You’ve been keeping track?”

She turned to me.

“I’ll let you know if the menu changes.”

I laughed again. She was smiling too, watching the dog carry its bread away from the doorway before eating it.

I had been ready to tell her about the afternoon: the three versions of the same file, the changes I’d been asked to undo, the call I would eventually have to return. Now it seemed like a long story to tell someone whose name I didn’t know.

I could tell her another time.

The thought came easily enough that I didn’t question it.

A bus came into view. She leaned forward to check the number.

“Here’s yours.”

I stood and put my bag over my shoulder.

“Are you getting on?”

“Not this one.”

The bus stopped. I had one foot on the step when I turned back.

“What’s your name?”

“Imaya.”

The conductor asked me to move along. I climbed aboard and found a seat by the window.

She was still on the bench. I raised my hand, and she gave a small nod before the bus pulled away.

Only then did I realise I hadn’t told her my name.

On my phone, the message I’d been writing to my manager was still unfinished. I read it through. Most of it was an argument about whose fault the delay had been.

I deleted it.

I’ll check the file and send it in the morning.

There would still be work waiting when I got home. But for the next few stops I watched the shops go by without rehearsing what I should have said in his office.

At home, I left my shoes by the door and put the kettle on. While I waited, I thought of the shopkeeper standing under his awning, astonished that anyone could object to being drenched.

I found myself smiling.

The following evening, I took my umbrella from the locker before leaving.

As I approached the bus stop, I could see someone sitting at the end of the bench. I recognised the tied-back hair first.

Imaya looked up.

“Did I miss it again?”

“Not yet.”

I sat beside her and kept my bag on my lap.

“It’s dry today,” she said.

“I’m not taking chances.”

Across the road, the awning was rolled up. The dog hadn’t arrived.

For a moment I considered asking whether she’d been waiting long, but it sounded as though I thought she’d been waiting for me.

“I didn’t introduce myself yesterday,” I said. “My name’s—”

“I know.”

I paused.

“My name?”

She nodded. Then she looked down at her hands, as though she hadn’t meant to interrupt.

“Have we met?”

She hesitated.

“I’ve seen you before.”
