Dear Stranger,

On a gloomy day in September I died. With me, the hope of true love and the trust of having a calm relationship died too. After I died, I had to collect my pieces and go to work the next day, answer emails, smile at people, pretend my chest wasn’t a demolition site. Funny how something I once held sacred turned out to be exactly what everyone warned me it was: fragile, conditional, temporary.

You’ve been asking about this. Not directly, but your little check-ins, the way you circle the subject without touching it, told me everything. So here it is.

What broke me wasn’t just losing someone. It was realizing I had been alone inside something I thought was mutual. I stayed soft when it would’ve been easier to go numb. And somehow, that became my crime. I wasn’t too much, I was just too real for someone who hadn’t made peace with their own mess.

The worst part wasn’t the ending. It was the slow erosion before it. The second-guessing. The shrinking. The way I started editing myself just to keep the peace. I confused patience with love, silence with maturity, and loneliness with strength. That’s on me, and I own it. But I also know now love isn’t supposed to feel like survival.

After September, I didn’t just grieve for a person. I grieved the version of myself who believed consistency meant safety, who thought effort guaranteed reciprocity, who trusted that good intentions could override emotional unavailability. That guy didn’t make it, I did. A Different version of myself who won't settle for basic care.

For months after, I walked around functioning but hollow. Writing helped. So did solitude. So did finally admitting I wasn’t okay instead of pretending I was handling it. Some days I missed them. Some days I missed who I was before them. Most days I just missed peace.

But here’s the truth I didn’t talk about: heartbreak doesn’t just hurt, it rewires you. It forces you to confront patterns you tolerated, boundaries you didn’t enforce, and the ways you abandoned yourself trying to keep someone else. That realization was brutal.

I’m not bitter, I wish I was. But that’s not the person I am. Instead I became clearer.

I stopped romanticising chaos.I used to do that often. I stopped over-explaining my needs hoping someone will finally understand them. If I’m not met halfway, I stop walking. I want to believe that’s what’s right for me. If there’s no one, then so be it.

So when you ask how I’m doing now? I’m alive. Not the same, but more honest. Less hopeful in a naive way. More hopeful in a grounded way. The kind that doesn’t need grand promises, just consistency, presence, and peace.

But, My Dear stranger, September killed a version of me. That might be the best thing that ever happened to me.
